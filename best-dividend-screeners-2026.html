What a Dividend Screener Actually Does

A screener takes thousands of stocks or funds and cuts them down to the handful that match your rules. That's it. It doesn't tell you what to buy. It tells you what's worth looking at.

The difference between a useful screener and a useless one comes down to the filters. Almost every free tool lets you filter by dividend yield. The better ones let you filter on the things that predict whether that dividend survives: payout ratio, years of dividend growth, free cash flow, and for funds, total return and whether the share price has been eroding.

The part most people miss: a 12% yield on a stock or fund whose price falls 10% a year isn't income. It's your own money coming back to you. A good screener lets you see the yield and the total return side by side so you catch that before you buy.

The Filters I Actually Use

For dividend stocks

  • A yield range, not "highest first." Something like 2% to 6% for most stocks. Above that, I want a very good reason.
  • Payout ratio ceiling. I like to see earnings or free cash flow covering the dividend comfortably. REITs and MLPs need different measures, so screen them separately.
  • Years of dividend growth. Five years minimum, ten is better. A long streak tells you management treats the dividend as a priority.
  • Positive free cash flow. Dividends get paid in cash, not adjusted earnings.
  • Minimum market cap. Cuts out a lot of tiny, illiquid names you'd struggle to research anyway.

For dividend and income ETFs

  • Total return, not just yield. If the total return since launch is weak or negative, the high yield didn't help anyone.
  • Price decay. Is the share price drifting lower over time? That's the classic sign a payout is too big for the strategy.
  • Expense ratio and fund size. Small, expensive funds are more likely to close.
  • Payout frequency. If you want a monthly or weekly paycheck, filter for it up front.

How I Screen in 10 Minutes

  1. Pick the universe. Stocks or ETFs? Don't mix them in one screen; the filters that matter are different.
  2. Set the yield range first, then add the quality filters above. Aim for a list of 10 to 25 names, not 300.
  3. Sort by something other than yield. Dividend growth streak for stocks, total return for ETFs.
  4. Check the top five by hand. Dividend history, payout coverage, and for funds, whether the price has held up. A good dividend calendar helps you see when each one pays.
  5. Track what you buy. Once you own something, move it into a tracker. Here's my list of the best free dividend trackers.

That's it. The screener's job is to save you time and keep you away from the obvious traps. Your job is the last 10% of judgment.

Mistakes I See All the Time

  • Sorting by highest yield. The top of that list is almost always where the cuts and the price decay live.
  • Ignoring total return. Income that comes with a shrinking share price is a bad trade over time. I'd rather own a 4% yielder that grows than chase a 20% yielder that bleeds.
  • Paying for three tools that do the same thing. Most people need one stock screener, one ETF tool and one tracker. That's the whole stack.
  • Never modeling the outcome. Before you commit, run the numbers in one of the best DRIP calculators to see what reinvesting actually does over 10 or 20 years.

FAQ: Dividend Screeners

What is the best free dividend screener in 2026?

For individual dividend stocks, Stock Unlock's free plan and StockAnalysis.com are the strongest free options. For dividend ETFs, TopDividendETFs.com is free and flags price decay, and Dividend Decoder is a free way to check whether a fund's payout looks sustainable.

What filters should I use on a dividend stock screener?

Start with a sensible yield range instead of sorting by highest yield, then add a payout ratio ceiling, a minimum number of years of dividend growth, positive free cash flow and a minimum market cap. That combination removes most yield traps before you research anything.

Is there a screener for dividend ETFs?

Yes. TopDividendETFsPRO screens 130+ income ETFs by live yield, total return, price decay and rating. YieldCanary focuses on covered call and option income ETFs, and Dividend Decoder covers 529 income funds including ETFs, REITs, BDCs and CEFs.

How do I find the best dividend stocks for 2026?

Run a screener with quality filters like payout ratio, dividend growth streak and free cash flow, cut the list to a handful of names, then check each company's dividend safety and history before buying.

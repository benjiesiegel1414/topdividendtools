How to Track Dividends in 2026: Spreadsheet vs App (Step by Step)

By Benjie • Published November 4, 2026 • TopDividendTools.com

The easiest way to track your dividends in 2026 is to pick one place, either a spreadsheet or a dividend tracker app, and log three things for every holding: shares owned, dividend per share and pay frequency. From that you get your annual income, your monthly average and your yield on cost. A spreadsheet is free and fully yours. An app like Snowball Analytics, Sharesight, The Dividend Tracker or MerryDiv can sync your brokerages and do the math for you.

I've tracked my dividend income for over ten years. It's how I watched the snowball build until my dividends covered my bills at 31. And here's the part most people miss: the tool matters a lot less than tracking the right numbers every month. Below is exactly how to set it up, both ways, with real 2026 pricing for the apps.

The Only Numbers You Actually Need to Track

Before you open any tool, know what you're trying to see. Most people track way too much and quit after three weeks. Here's what I actually look at:

  • Forward annual income. Shares times the annual dividend per share, added up across everything you own. This is the big one. It tells you what your portfolio pays you if nothing changes.
  • Monthly average income. Forward annual income divided by 12. Compare it to your monthly bills and you know how close you are to dividend freedom.
  • Dividends actually received. What hit your account, by month. Projections are nice; cash received is the truth.
  • Yield on cost. Annual income divided by what you paid. It shows how your income grows on money you put in years ago.
  • Price decay on high-income funds. If you own covered call ETFs, note whether the share price is holding up. A 12% yield on a fund that bleeds 15% a year is not income.

That's it. Five numbers. Anything else is a bonus.

Option 1: Track Dividends in a Spreadsheet (Free)

This is how I started, and I still think every dividend investor should do it once. You learn exactly where your income comes from, and nobody can raise the price on you.

Step 1: Set up your columns

Open Google Sheets or Excel and make one row per holding with these columns:

Step 2: Let the price update itself

In Google Sheets, =GOOGLEFINANCE("SCHD","price") pulls a delayed price for most U.S. tickers. That keeps your portfolio value current without typing anything.

Dividends are trickier. Google Sheets doesn't give you a reliable dividend history for ETFs, so I'd enter the trailing 12-month dividend per share yourself and refresh it every quarter. Free sites like StockAnalysis show every payment, and I cover more of them in the best dividend websites roundup.

Step 3: Add a monthly income log

Make a second tab with one row per month and one column for each account. Every time a dividend lands, add it. At the end of the year you have a chart of real income, not projections, and it's the most motivating chart you'll ever look at.

Step 4: Update once a month

Pick one day a month. Update share counts (DRIP adds fractional shares quietly), log what you received, and look at your monthly average. Ten minutes. Done.

Common Dividend Tracking Mistakes

  • Forgetting DRIP shares. Reinvested dividends buy fractional shares. Skip them and your income projection drifts lower every quarter.
  • Using a single payment as the annual number. Monthly and weekly payers change their payout constantly. Use the trailing 12 months, not the last check times 12.
  • Counting yield, ignoring total return. Tracking only income can hide a fund whose share price keeps falling. Check total return once a quarter on ETFTotalReturns.com.
  • Tracking accounts in different places. Your income is one number. Keep the IRA, 401(k) and taxable account in the same tracker.
  • Not knowing when the cash lands. A calendar view helps you plan around it. See the best dividend calendars.

FAQ: How to Track Your Dividends

What is the best way to track your dividends?

Pick one place and log shares, annual dividend per share and pay frequency for every holding. A spreadsheet works well for small portfolios at one broker. If you have several accounts, a dividend tracker app with brokerage sync, like Snowball Analytics, Sharesight, The Dividend Tracker or MerryDiv, saves time.

Which portfolio trackers offer the best dividend tracking features?

Look for forward income projections, a dividend calendar, automatic DRIP tracking and brokerage sync. Snowball Analytics and Sharesight are the most complete, The Dividend Tracker is built only for dividends, and MerryDiv focuses on a monthly income view across accounts.

Can I track dividends in Google Sheets?

Yes. Make one row per holding with shares, annual dividend per share and a formula for annual income. GOOGLEFINANCE can update prices automatically, but you should enter dividend amounts yourself from a dividend history site, since Google Sheets does not reliably supply ETF dividends.

Is there a free dividend tracker?

Yes. Snowball Analytics, Sharesight and The Dividend Tracker all have free plans capped at about 10 holdings, MerryDiv and Yieldos have free plans, and a spreadsheet is always free.

How often should I update my dividend tracker?

Once a month is enough for most investors. Update share counts after DRIP purchases, log the dividends you received and check your monthly average income.

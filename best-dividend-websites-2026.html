Best Free Websites for Dividend History and Dates

1. StockAnalysis.com

If I could only keep one free dividend data site, this might be it. Pull up any stock or ETF and the dividend tab shows the yield, the annual payout, the payout frequency and a clean table of every ex-date, amount and pay date. The ETF holdings tab shows the top holdings and weights, which is something a lot of free sites hide.

The free version covers most of what a self-directed investor needs. Pro, at $9.99 a month or $79 a year, adds longer history and exports. I ranked it as the best free dividend data source in my guide to the best dividend screeners.

Use it for: "what did this fund pay, and when?"

2. Nasdaq.com

Nasdaq's dividend history pages list the declaration date, ex-date, record date and payment date for each payout. That declaration date is handy. It tells you when the company or fund actually announced the payment, which is the first moment the number is real.

Use it for: double-checking dates before you buy around an ex-dividend date.

3. Yahoo Finance

Everyone has used it. It's fast for a quick quote, the forward dividend and yield, and the next ex-date. Just know that the yield you see on any quote site is calculated one specific way, and it won't always match the issuer or other sites. That's the part most people miss. Two sites showing different yields usually aren't wrong. They're measuring different things, like trailing 12 months versus the latest payment annualized.

Use it for: a 10-second check, not deep research.

Here's what I actually look at first: the last 12 months of payments in dollars, not the yield percentage. The dollar amounts are facts. The yield depends on the price and the method. If the dollars per share keep growing, I'm happy. If they're shrinking while the yield looks huge, that's a warning.

5. Fund issuer pages and SEC EDGAR

Not exciting, but this is the source of truth. The issuer's fund page has the official distribution history, the expense ratio, the holdings and the prospectus. For funds that pay part of their distribution from something other than income, look for the Section 19(a) notices. They estimate how much of a payment is return of capital, which you won't see on most quote sites.

SEC EDGAR has every official filing for free. You won't use it every day, but when two sites disagree, go here.

Use it for: settling disagreements and checking where a big yield really comes from.

Best Websites for Tracking Your Dividend Income

6. Snowball Analytics

Data sites tell you about funds. A tracker tells you about your money: what you'll be paid this month, your yield on cost, and how your income is growing. Snowball is still my default pick for most dividend investors. The free plan covers 1 portfolio and 10 holdings, and paid plans start at $79.99 a year.

I've compared it in detail in Snowball Analytics alternatives and in my roundup of the best dividend portfolio trackers. If you want something free, start with the best free dividend tracker list.

Use it for: watching your income grow and seeing every upcoming payment.

Best Websites for Researching Dividend Stocks

7. Seeking Alpha Premium

If you buy individual stocks, Seeking Alpha Premium gives you Dividend Grades for safety, growth, yield and consistency, plus Quant Ratings, earnings call transcripts and a big screener. The list price is about $299 a year, and discounts are common. The downside is noise. Thousands of authors means you get great analysis and a lot of opinions you have to filter.

8. Simply Safe Dividends

Its Dividend Safety Scores are probably the best-known dividend cut warning system. It costs $468 a year, billed annually. Worth it if you own dozens of individual dividend stocks and live off that income. Probably not if you mostly own ETFs. I broke down cheaper options in Simply Safe Dividends alternatives.

Use these for: deciding whether a single company's dividend is safe.

Another one of mine. DividendProjection.com is a free calculator: pick an ETF, an amount and a monthly deposit, turn reinvesting on or off, and see what it could pay you year by year. It's useful for the "how much do I need?" question. For example, I just walked through the VIG dividend history and calculator, which shows why a low starting yield can still grow into real income.

My Simple Three-Site Setup

If you want to keep it simple, this is what I'd suggest for most self-directed income investors:

  • One data site: StockAnalysis for history and holdings, with the issuer page as the final word.
  • One comparison site: TopDividendETFs.com free, or TopDividendETFsPRO if you want the filters.
  • One tracker: Snowball, or a free tracker if you're just starting.

That's it. After 10+ years of building dividend income, I can tell you the site doesn't make the money. Buying good funds, reinvesting and staying consistent does. The websites just help you avoid dumb mistakes, like chasing a 40% yield that's eating its own share price.

FAQ: Best Dividend Websites

What is the best free dividend website?

For free dividend history and dates, StockAnalysis.com and Nasdaq.com are the strongest picks. For comparing dividend ETFs and spotting price decay, TopDividendETFs.com is free. For the official numbers, use the fund issuer's own page.

What is the best dividend tracking website?

Snowball Analytics is the best all-around dividend tracker for most investors. It has a free plan with 1 portfolio and 10 holdings, and paid plans start at $79.99 a year with income projections, a dividend calendar and broker syncing.

Is there a website to compare dividend ETFs?

Yes. TopDividendETFs.com ranks dividend ETFs for free and flags price decay. TopDividendETFsPRO adds advanced filtering, ratings, live yields and total returns for 130+ income ETFs, from $12.95 a month or lifetime access.

Why do dividend websites show different yields?

Sites calculate yield in different ways, such as the last 12 months of payments divided by price, or the latest payment annualized. Both can be correct. Compare the dollar amount paid per share over the last year instead, and check the issuer's page for the official distributions.

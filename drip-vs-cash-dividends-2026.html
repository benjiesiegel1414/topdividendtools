DRIP vs Cash Dividends 2026: Should You Reinvest or Take the Income?

By Benjie • Published November 22, 2026 • TopDividendTools.com

If you don't need the income yet, reinvesting your dividends (DRIP) almost always builds more wealth and more future income than taking cash. In the example below, the same $100,000 ends up paying about $16,490 a year after 20 years with DRIP, versus $8,844 a year if you took the cash. Once you actually need the money to live on, or you hold funds you shouldn't be adding to, taking the cash becomes the smarter move.

That's the short answer. The longer answer is where most people get tripped up, because DRIP vs cash isn't really a yes or no setting in your brokerage account. It's a decision about what each dollar of income should do next. I've been a dividend investor for over ten years and I've used both approaches at different stages, so here's how I actually think about it.

What DRIP Actually Means

DRIP stands for dividend reinvestment plan. When a stock or ETF you own pays a dividend, your broker uses that cash to buy more shares of the same holding automatically, usually on the pay date. Most big brokers, including Fidelity, Schwab and Vanguard, do this for free and buy fractional shares, so every cent goes back to work.

Taking cash is the opposite. The dividend lands in your account as cash and sits there until you decide what to do with it: spend it, move it to savings, or invest it somewhere else.

Here's the part most people miss: those are not the only two choices. There's a third option, and for a lot of investors it's the best one. I'll get to it below.

The Math: DRIP vs Cash Over 20 Years

Let's use a simple, hypothetical example so you can see the difference. You put $100,000 into a dividend fund with a 3.5% starting yield. The share price grows 5% a year and the dividend grows 5% a year. No taxes, no new money, nothing fancy. One investor reinvests every dividend. The other takes every dividend as cash and doesn't reinvest it.

Look at year 20. The DRIP investor has about $511,000 and earns $16,490 a year. The cash investor has about $265,000 in the fund plus $115,731 they collected along the way, roughly $381,000 combined, and earns $8,844 a year.

So DRIP wins by about $130,000 in total wealth and almost doubles the annual income. That's compounding: new shares pay their own dividends, which buy more shares, which pay more dividends.

But notice something else. The cash investor didn't lose $115,731. They got it. If that money paid for 20 years of real life, that's not a failure. That's the point of income investing. The question is only whether you needed it.

My rule of thumb: every dollar of income I don't need for bills should be working. Whether it goes back into the same fund is a separate question.

When DRIP Makes the Most Sense

  • You're still building. If you have a job covering your bills and the dividends are a bonus, reinvesting is the easiest way to grow your future income without thinking about it.
  • The holding is one you'd buy more of anyway. A solid dividend growth ETF or a broad index fund you plan to hold for decades is the ideal DRIP candidate.
  • You'd otherwise let the cash sit. Be honest here. If dividends pile up as uninvested cash for months, DRIP fixes that behavior problem automatically.
  • It's in a retirement account. In an IRA or 401(k), reinvesting doesn't create a tax bill or a bookkeeping mess, so there's very little downside.

When Taking Cash Is the Better Move

  • You live on the income. This is the obvious one. When I reached the point where dividends covered my expenses, the whole reason for owning them changed. Income you need should hit your account as cash.
  • The fund has price decay. This is the big one for high-yield ETFs. If a fund's share price keeps sliding because it pays out more than it earns, auto-reinvesting just buys more of a shrinking asset. I'd rather take that cash and decide where it goes.
  • A position is already too big. DRIP keeps adding to whatever you own, including the holdings that have grown into a big chunk of your portfolio. Taking cash lets you rebalance without selling.
  • You want to pick your entry. Some investors like to collect cash and buy whatever looks best that month. That works, as long as you actually do it.

The Third Option: Take Cash, Then Redirect It

This is what I think more income investors should do. Turn DRIP off, let the dividends land as cash, and once a month put that cash into whatever you want to own more of.

You keep most of the compounding from DRIP, because the money is still being reinvested. But you control where it goes. Dividends from a high-yield covered call fund can flow into a dividend growth fund. Income from an overweight position can flow into an underweight one. It's DRIP with a steering wheel.

The only real cost is discipline. If you know you won't do it, leave DRIP on. Automatic beats perfect.

Taxes: DRIP Doesn't Save You Anything

This one surprises people. In a regular taxable brokerage account, reinvested dividends are taxed exactly the same as dividends you take in cash. The IRS treats the dividend as income in the year it's paid, whether you spent it or bought more shares with it. Your broker reports it on your 1099-DIV either way.

The good news: each reinvested dividend adds to your cost basis. Brokers track that for you now, but it matters when you eventually sell, because you don't want to pay tax twice on the same money. If you use an older plan held directly with a company or transfer agent, keep your own records.

And if a fund pays a lot of return of capital, which is common with some option income ETFs, that portion generally isn't taxed when paid but lowers your cost basis instead. Another reason to know what you own before you put it on autopilot.

The Bottom Line

If you don't need the income, reinvest it. That's the single biggest lever most dividend investors have, and the math isn't close over 10 or 20 years.

If you do need the income, take the cash and don't feel bad about it. That's what you built it for.

And if you're somewhere in between, or you own any high-yield funds, my favorite approach is the middle one: take the cash, then send it where it does the most good. Compounding plus control. That's how I'd rather run an income portfolio than letting every fund feed itself.

FAQ: DRIP vs Cash Dividends

Is it better to reinvest dividends or take cash?

If you don't need the income yet, reinvesting usually builds more wealth and more future income. In a hypothetical $100,000 example with a 3.5% yield and 5% growth, DRIP paid about $16,490 a year after 20 years versus $8,844 with cash. If you live on the income or hold funds with price decay, taking cash is often the better choice.

Are reinvested dividends taxed?

Yes. In a taxable brokerage account, reinvested dividends are taxed the same as dividends taken in cash, in the year they are paid, and they show up on your 1099-DIV. Each reinvested dividend adds to your cost basis. In an IRA or 401(k), dividends are not taxed when paid.

Should retirees turn off DRIP?

Many retirees turn off DRIP on the income they need to cover expenses, so dividends land as cash. If some of the income is not needed, you can keep reinvesting that part or redirect the cash into whatever you want to own more of.

Should I DRIP high-yield or covered call ETFs?

Be careful. If a high-yield fund has price decay, reinvesting just buys more of a shrinking asset. Check its total return first. Many income investors take the cash from these funds and redirect it into holdings with better total returns.

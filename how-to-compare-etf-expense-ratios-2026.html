How to Compare ETF Expense Ratios in 2026 (Free Tools + the Real Math)

By Benjie • Published November 26, 2026 • TopDividendTools.com

To compare ETF expense ratios, pull the total (gross and net) expense ratio from each fund's own page or prospectus fee table, line them up side by side, and convert the percentage into dollars on your actual balance over your actual time horizon. A free tool like FINRA's Fund Analyzer does that dollar math for you. The part most people skip: make sure you're comparing funds that do the same job, and that you're reading the total expense ratio, not just the management fee.

A version of this question keeps bringing people to this site: how do you compare ETF expense ratios? It sounds simple. It mostly is. But I've watched a lot of income investors get it wrong in both directions, either obsessing over 0.02% on an index fund or ignoring a 1.5% fee on a high-yield fund because the yield looked so good. What an Expense Ratio Actually Is

The expense ratio is the yearly cost of running the fund, shown as a percentage of your investment. You never get a bill for it. The fund takes it out of its assets a little at a time, so it shows up as slightly lower returns and slightly smaller distributions.

The math is just your balance times the ratio. A 0.06% expense ratio on $10,000 is about $6 a year. A 0.60% expense ratio on $10,000 is about $60 a year. A 1.52% expense ratio is about $152 a year. Same $10,000, 25 times the cost between the cheapest and the most expensive.

That's why "it's only 1%" is a dangerous sentence. On a $500,000 portfolio, 1% is $5,000 a year, every year, whether the fund has a good year or not.

Notice the jumps. Plain index funds cost a few dollars a year. Covered call funds cost tens of dollars. Funds that hold other funds can cost over $100. None of that is automatically wrong. An option income fund has real work to do every week. But you need to know what you're paying for.

The Real Math: What Fees Cost Over 20 Years

The yearly dollar amount understates the damage, because fees compound too. Every dollar taken out of the fund is a dollar that never grows.

Here's a simple example. $10,000, a hypothetical 8% yearly return before fees, 20 years, nothing added. The only thing that changes is the expense ratio.

Between 0.03% and 0.06%, the difference over 20 years is a nice dinner. Between 0.03% and 1.33%, it's about $10,000, which is the entire amount you started with. That's the whole lesson in one table: small differences between cheap funds barely matter, big differences between cheap and expensive funds matter a lot.

How to Compare ETF Expense Ratios in 5 Steps

  1. Compare funds that do the same job. $IVV at 0.03% vs $SPYI at 0.68% isn't a fair fee comparison. One is a plain index fund, the other writes options for income. Compare an S&P 500 fund to other S&P 500 funds, and a covered call fund to other covered call funds.
  2. Get the number from the issuer. The fund's own page and the fee table in its prospectus are the source. Data sites are great for scanning, but they can lag a fee change.
  3. Read the total, not the headline fee. Look for "total expense ratio," "total annual fund operating expenses" or "gross expense ratio." The management fee alone can be a lot smaller (more on that below).
  4. Check gross vs net. If a fund lists a lower net expense ratio, that usually means a fee waiver. Find out when the waiver ends, because the fee can go back up.
  5. Turn it into dollars over your time horizon. Your balance times the ratio gives you the yearly cost. A tool like FINRA's Fund Analyzer shows the cost compounded over the years you plan to hold.

The Traps: Where the Headline Fee Doesn't Tell the Whole Story

1. Management fee vs total expense ratio

This is the big one. $YMAX lists a management fee of 0.29% on its fund page. Its gross expense ratio is 1.33%. Why the gap? $YMAX is a fund of funds. It owns other YieldMax ETFs, and their fees get counted too (these are called acquired fund fees). YieldMax says directly that owning $YMAX entails more costs than owning the underlying funds directly.

If you only glanced at the 0.29%, you'd think it was cheaper than $JEPI. It's almost four times more expensive.

2. Gross vs net (fee waivers)

Some funds show two numbers. Gross is the full cost. Net is what you pay after the issuer agrees to waive part of it. Waivers are fine, but they're usually temporary. When I compare, I note both and assume the gross number could come back.

3. Trading costs that aren't in the ratio

The expense ratio doesn't include the bid-ask spread you pay when you buy and sell. On giant funds it's tiny. On small, newer income ETFs it can be meaningful. For example, REX lists a 0.31% median bid-ask spread for its $TSII weekly Tesla income fund, which also has a 1.52% gross expense ratio. If you trade often, that spread is a second fee. Use limit orders on small funds.

4. Yield that hides the fee

For income funds, the distribution you see is after expenses. That sounds like good news, but it means a high-fee fund can still show a big yield while slowly eating its own share price. The fee is one drag. Price decay is another. That's why I never judge an income ETF on yield and fee alone. I check total return.

My rule: for index funds, I pick the cheapest one that tracks what I want and stop thinking about it. For income funds, I'll pay a higher fee only if the total return after fees shows the strategy is worth it.

When a Higher Expense Ratio Is Worth Paying

I'm not saying always buy the cheapest fund. I'm saying know what you're getting for the extra cost.

  • Paying more can make sense when the fund does something you can't easily do yourself, like run a daily or weekly options strategy, and its total return after fees holds up against a cheap alternative over the same dates.
  • Paying more makes no sense when two funds track the same index and one simply charges more. That's money for nothing.
  • Be extra careful with funds of funds and small, new funds. Layered fees and wide spreads add up quietly.

The Bottom Line

Comparing ETF expense ratios takes five minutes. Get the total expense ratio from the issuer, compare funds that do the same job, watch for the management fee vs total fee trap and temporary waivers, and turn the percentage into dollars over the years you plan to hold. For cheap index funds, the differences barely matter. For income funds charging 0.6% to 1.5%, they matter a lot, and the only fair test is whether the total return after fees is worth it. That's how I'd rather think about fees: not as a number to minimize at all costs, but as a price that has to be earned.

FAQ: Comparing ETF Expense Ratios

How do you compare ETF expense ratios?

Get the total expense ratio for each fund from the issuer's fund page or prospectus fee table, compare only funds that do the same job, check gross vs net for temporary fee waivers, and convert the percentage into dollars on your balance over your holding period. FINRA's free Fund Analyzer does the dollar math for you.

What is a good expense ratio for an ETF?

It depends on the type of fund. Broad index ETFs can cost 0.03% to 0.10%, like the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF at 0.03% or the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF at 0.06%. Covered call and option income ETFs usually cost more, often 0.35% to 0.70%, and some funds of funds and single-stock income ETFs charge well over 1%.

What is the difference between the management fee and the expense ratio?

The management fee is just one part of the cost. The total expense ratio adds other expenses, including acquired fund fees when an ETF owns other funds. For example, YieldMax lists a 0.29% management fee for its $YMAX fund of funds but a 1.33% gross expense ratio.

Is there a free tool to compare ETF fees?

Yes. FINRA's Fund Analyzer is free and compares fees for more than 30,000 mutual funds, ETFs, ETNs and money market funds, showing how costs affect a fund's value over time. StockAnalysis also has a free side-by-side ETF compare that includes the expense ratio.

Is the expense ratio taken out of ETF dividends?

The expense ratio is deducted from the fund's assets over the year rather than billed to you, so it shows up as slightly lower returns and lower distributions. The yield you see on an ETF is already after expenses, which is why a high-fee fund can still show a big yield while its share price declines.

What I Personally Love About MerryDiv

1. It works with your actual brokerage accounts, safely

This is the big one for me. I don't want to type in every buy, every DRIP and every dividend by hand. With MerryDiv you connect your brokerages through Plaid, the same connection service a lot of banking and budgeting apps use, and your holdings and dividend payments sync in on their own.

The connection is read-only. MerryDiv can see your positions and payouts, but it can't trade, move money or change anything in your account, and your brokerage login goes through Plaid instead of being stored on MerryDiv's servers. That's exactly how I want a tracker to work. It covers Schwab, Fidelity, Vanguard, Robinhood, E*TRADE, Merrill Edge and thousands more, and you can mix a Roth IRA, 401(k), taxable account and HSA into one dividend feed.

2. Simple to follow, but it looks premium

A lot of trackers either look like a spreadsheet from 2009 or bury your income under ten charts you'll never use. MerryDiv gets the balance right. You open it and the first thing you see is what you actually care about: your average monthly dividend income, what you've received lately, and what's coming next.

It's clean and easy enough for a brand new investor, but the design is polished. Little touches like the "journey to freedom" progress bar toward your monthly income goal and the "this paycheck bought" card make it feel like a tool someone cared about building. I found myself opening it just to look at it.

3. The dividend data is accurate

Here's something most people don't realize until they've used a few trackers: a lot of dividend trackers show you the wrong numbers. They estimate your income from a generic dividend database, so special dividends get missed, DRIP shares get counted wrong, ETF distributions that change every month get guessed at, and your "income" ends up off from what your broker actually paid you.

MerryDiv takes a different approach. According to MerryDiv, your dividend payments and history come directly from your brokerage via Plaid, so they match what actually hit your account. For yields, stocks use the indicated forward yield and funds use trailing 12-month cash distributions, which is the right way to look at monthly-paying income ETFs. When I'm tracking real income, real numbers are the whole point.

Why this matters for ETF investors: If you hold monthly payers like $JEPI, $JEPQ or $SPYI, the distribution changes almost every month. A tracker that projects from an old number will be wrong. A tracker that records what your broker actually paid won't be.
